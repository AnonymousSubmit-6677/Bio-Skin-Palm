Bio-Skin-Palm: An Open-Source Multimodal Tactile Sensor for the Palm of Dexterous Robotic Hands

Anonymous Authors

Paper under double-blind review

Abstract

Palm tactile sensing is usually built as a larger, coarser copy of fingertip force sensing, and is usually evaluated only from the instant of contact onward. We argue that what distinguishes the palm is temporal rather than spatial: it is the one surface that faces the object across the entire interaction, so it should be asked questions the fingertips cannot answer at all.

Bio-Skin-Palm is a multimodal palm covering all three stages of a grasp — two 8 × 8 time-of-flight arrays for pre-contact geometry, a 15-channel Hall normal-force array with a triaxial shear channel and a 12-zone force-sensitive-resistor array for contact, and an actively heated thermal channel for post-contact material discrimination. Dense in-hand actuation makes Hall sensing magnetically fragile, so we pair a passive permalloy layer with FSR-gated cross-modal bias compensation, and report the resulting trade-off as measured rather than as hoped. Each stage is evaluated in isolation, and the three are evaluated together in a long-horizon, closed-loop grasping task.

Stage 1 — Pre-contactVision-degraded teleoperation

An external camera loses the object at the moment the hand closes around it, and an opaque bag removes the scene before that. Five operators teleoperated the hand into the bag using only the two palm ToF arrays, composited into the lower left of each clip: a 0–1000 mm panel for approach context and a 0–100 mm panel for the last few centimetres.

Across the five operators, all 36 objects were identified and 30 of 36 were lifted (83.3 %, 66.7–100 % per operator). Clips play at 10× by default; switch to 1× for the real pace of the search.

Each clip is one uncut session. The external camera is for the reader only — the operator never saw it.

Stage 2 — ContactForce sensing under magnetic interference

The closed loop reads Hall normal force directly to decide when to stop closing, so this stage has to show the reading stays trustworthy inside a hand full of servos. A passive permalloy layer flattens local field hot-spots, and FSR-gated cross-modal compensation removes the residual bias only while the corresponding zone reports no contact.

On the triaxial shear channel, which has no magnetically immune reference and therefore no active fallback, shielding is a net gain: static offset −36.7 % and dynamic RMS error −33.2 %. Across the 15-channel normal array it redistributes rather than removes the disturbance.

Stage 3 — Post-contactThermal material recognition

Once size, shape and colour match, geometry stops discriminating. Three 60 mm cubes — machined metal, printed PLA, and the same PLA cube wrapped in 1 mm of paper — are indistinguishable to RGB and to the ToF point cloud alike. The palm presses each one, holds the contact for 10 s while the on-board heater maintains a temperature difference, and reads the local cooling rate.

Left: the contact sequence. Right: temperature change ΔT and local cooling rate against time, mean with spread, with the pre-contact baseline and the moment of contact marked.

All three stages togetherLong-horizon closed-loop grasping

A scripted scan builds a point cloud, a locally deployed VLM counts and selects clusters, the palm descends until either ToF or Hall force fires the stopping criterion, and — for the two visually identical cubes — the thermal criterion chooses the target before the fingers close on it.

The box track succeeded in 18 of 20 trials (90.0 %) under the full criterion of grasp plus 30 cm lift; the cube track succeeded in 16 of 20 (80.0 %) under the partial criterion of correct material plus finger closure. The two criteria differ, so the rates are deliberately not merged.
